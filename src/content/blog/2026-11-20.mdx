## An Apple a Day

I recently made a [post on my LinkedIn](https://lnkd.in/p/gEtWFG6H) that got some attention. It was
a picture of my racked Mac Minis that power my digital life, and a brief overview of what I'm using
them for. I got some asks for a blog post discussing it further, and I'm more than happy to oblige
:)

I started hoarding M4 [Mac Minis](https://www.apple.com/mac-mini/) at the beginning of 2025, back
when the base model Mac Mini would set you back $599. Since then, my homelab's macOS footprint has
grown from one node to six, with a full suite of infrastructure-as-code tooling behind it.

Nearly two years in, I've gotten into a rhythm working with them, and I have plenty of thoughts
(`and code!`) to share.

## Bearing Fruition

Back in 1999, Apple ran [an ad](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OoxvLq0dFvw) bragging that the
[Power Mac G4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_Mac_G4) was so powerful it counted as a
supercomputer under US export rules, complete with tanks rolling in to guard it. Makes me wonder how
a rack full of Macs working together as an actual cluster would be classified.

Part of why I went down this path was curiosity about Apple Silicon's performance per watt. A Mac
Mini sips power at idle, runs nearly silent, and still has a Media Engine for video, a GPU that
speaks Metal, and a Neural Engine for CoreML. For a homelab that's on 24/7, and for someone who's
familiar with Apple products, that's a pretty compelling package to put in a rack.

### The Network

My homelab uses [UniFi](https://ui.com) networking throughout. The
[Dream Machine Pro](https://store.ui.com/us/en/products/udm-pro) handles routing and doubles as the
console for managing everything remotely. The
[Aggregation switch](https://store.ui.com/us/en/products/usw-aggregation) provides the 10Gbit
backbone, and a [24-port switch](https://store.ui.com/us/en/products/usw-24) handles everything that
doesn't need the extra bandwidth.

My favorite [Ubiquiti Networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ubiquiti) fun fact is that founder
[Robert Pera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Pera) had worked at Apple before starting the
company, so I found it fitting to bring hardware from those two companies together.

### The Compute

Six Mac Minis live in Sonnet's [RackMac Mini](https://www.sonnettech.com/product/rackmacmini.html)
enclosures, three to a shelf, each labeled with its hostname. The Minis are split into two Nomad
datacenters:

- **Cosmonautical** - _the core cluster_
  - Runs Nomad and Consul as server-client combos
  - Hosts everything I self-host, from Postgres to Jellyfin
- **Jellify** - _client-only_
  - Runs
    [GitHub Actions runners](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/hosting-your-own-runners/managing-self-hosted-runners/about-self-hosted-runners)
    for [Jellify](https://jellify.app), the music player for Jellyfin that I work on
    - [Xcode](https://developer.apple.com/xcode/), [Maestro](https://maestro.mobile.dev),
      simulators, and the [Android SDK](https://developer.android.com/studio) are all provisioned by
      Ansible, so a fresh Mac goes from unboxed to building and testing apps with a single playbook
      run.
  - Also home to a few x64 Linux nodes for the jobs that don't need a Mac

### The Storage

Bulk SSD storage lives on a [UNAS Pro 8](https://store.ui.com/us/en/products/unas-pro-8), which
serves [NFS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_File_System) shares (music, books, ROMs and so
on) to the whole cluster. A DIY Thunderbolt DAS holds 10 hard drives and is attached directly to
Cassiopeia.

## Software Sells Hardware

Hardware is nothing without the software to leverage it. Running bleeding-edge, proprietary hardware
does limit the fun, but as you might imagine there is still plenty of fun to be had.

### The OS: macOS

The software base is, of course, [macOS](https://www.apple.com/macos/). Linux _does_ support Apple
Silicon thanks to the people way smarter than me working on [Asahi Linux](https://asahilinux.org),
but it [doesn't support M4 chips](https://asahilinux.org/docs/platform/feature-support/m4/) - at
least as of this writing.

macOS is very much designed for the desktop, and even daily driving a MacBook Pro didn't prepare me
for running it headless. Most of the challenges come down to macOS assuming there's a person sitting
in front of the screen, ready to click "Allow".

**[Local Network privacy](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technotes/tn3179-understanding-local-network-privacy)
is the Achilles' heel.** Since [macOS Sequoia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacOS_Sequoia), apps
need permission to talk to devices on the local network, and that permission is granted through a
GUI prompt. That's great on a laptop, but not on a headless server. It's tied to the binary's code
signature, so every time [Homebrew](https://brew.sh) upgrades a service like `consul` or `php`,
macOS treats the new binary as a stranger and wants a fresh click. As far as I can tell this can't
be scripted either, since the permissions database is protected by
[System Integrity Protection](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102149). It's pretty frustrating: the
daemon logs `no route to host` while `nc` and `ssh` to the exact same IP work just fine.

This hasn't been a dealbreaker for me, but it means I'm using remote desktop /
[Guacamole](https://guacamole.apache.org) after upgrades to ensure that everything is connecting
properly.

### The Orchestrator: Nomad

With multiple nodes running, I wanted to have an orchestrator to keep everything in check. Before
the Macs, my homelab ran on a 3-node [Rancher Harvester](https://harvesterhci.io) cluster, so
[Kubernetes](https://kubernetes.io) was what I reached for first. The problem is that Kubernetes is
built and designed with Linux in mind, relying on Linux primitives to do its job.

I _could_ have run a Linux VM atop macOS to make this work, but that's an extra layer of complexity
on every node and it walls workloads off from the things that are fun about Apple Silicon in the
first place: the Media Engine, [Metal](https://developer.apple.com/metal/), and
[CoreML](https://developer.apple.com/machine-learning/core-ml/).

[Nomad](https://www.nomadproject.io) ended up being a perfect fit. It's a single macOS binary, and
its [`raw_exec`](https://developer.hashicorp.com/nomad/docs/job-declare/task-driver/raw_exec) driver
can schedule jobs straight on the host. That means [Jellyfin](https://jellyfin.org) gets hardware
transcoding, [Ollama](https://ollama.com) gets Metal, and CoreML apps get the Neural Engine, all
without any GPU passthrough to set up. Nice.

Nomad is also multi-platform, so the cluster isn't locked into macOS. I've got Linux nodes in the
Jellify datacenter now, and if I ever want a Windows box, it can join too.

#### The Container Runtime: Apple Container

Not everything I run ships as a native macOS app, though. A lot of apps I deploy only come as an OCI
image, and I need a container runtime to bring them online. The obvious choice is Docker; however,
on macOS that usually means running a full install of
[Docker Desktop](https://www.docker.com/products/docker-desktop/), or something like
[Colima](https://github.com/abiosoft/colima). [Podman](https://podman.io) is also an option, and
even has a [task driver for Nomad](https://github.com/hashicorp/nomad-driver-podman). I opted
instead to write my own task driver for Apple's [`container`](https://github.com/apple/container)
tool, which runs each Linux container in its own lightweight VM on the
[Virtualization framework](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/virtualization).

Both Docker and Podman work the same way on the Mac: they boot one big Linux VM in the background
and run every container inside of it. That VM is a box I have to size up front, carving out CPU and
memory whether anything is running or not, and when it gets wedged, every container on that node
goes down with it. It's also one more thing for Nomad to know nothing about, since all it sees is a
CLI talking to a daemon that's talking to a VM.

Apple's `container` flips that around. There's no shared VM to babysit; each container _is_ its own
VM, booting in about a second and sized to what the job asks for. When a Nomad allocation stops, its
VM goes away and the memory goes back to macOS, ready for another job to use. Every container also
gets its own IP address, so there's no port-forwarding dance between the host and a VM, and one
misbehaving container can't take its neighbors out.

It's also just... it's Apple's. It's written in [Swift](https://www.swift.org) and built on the same
frameworks as the rest of the OS, so it gets better with every macOS release instead of fighting
against it. On a cluster that's all-in on Apple Silicon, using Apple's own container runtime felt
like the natural fit. It's even open source.

If you'd like to try the driver out for yourself, you can grab the latest release from
[my GitHub repository](https://github.com/anultravioletaurora/nomad-driver-container/releases).
Feature requests welcome :)

#### The Service Mesh: Consul

Naturally, Nomad pairs nicely with [Consul](https://www.consul.io) for service discovery, health
checks, and key/value storage. Every service registers itself with Consul, so jobs find each other
by name over DNS (`<service>.service.consul`) instead of hard-coded IPs.

It pulls double duty, too: Consul is the distributed config store that
[Patroni](https://github.com/patroni/patroni) uses to handle Postgres failover, and it's where the
[Terraform state](https://developer.hashicorp.com/terraform/language/backend/consul) for my Nomad
jobs lives.

### The Management: Ansible

Setting up six Macs by hand was never going to happen. I'm far too lazy for that.

[Ansible](https://www.ansible.com) was the obvious choice for managing this six-pack. It runs over
SSH and only needs Python on the target, which the
[Xcode Command Line Tools](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/xcode/installing-the-command-line-tools)
provide. There's no agent to install and nothing else to bootstrap beyond flipping on
[Remote Login](https://support.apple.com/guide/mac-help/allow-a-remote-computer-to-access-your-mac-mchlp1066/mac).

Since adopting Ansible as my management tool of choice, I've built up quite a collection of
collections. They've grown into a nice lil family and are
[all published on Ansible Galaxy](https://galaxy.ansible.com/ui/namespaces/cosmonautical/):

- [`cosmonautical.macible`](https://github.com/Cosmonautical-Cloud/Macible): generic macOS roles for
  any Mac, server or workstation. Software Update, Homebrew, Xcode, the Android SDK, NFS mounts,
  container runtimes, etc.
- [`cosmonautical.nomadintosh`](https://github.com/Cosmonautical-Cloud/Nomadintosh): Nomad and
  Consul on macOS, built on top of Macible. Uses Homebrew for the binaries, and bootstraps
  [LaunchAgents](https://developer.apple.com/library/archive/documentation/MacOSX/Conceptual/BPSystemStartup/Chapters/CreatingLaunchdJobs.html)
  to keep them running.
- [`cosmonautical.nomaduntu`](https://github.com/Cosmonautical-Cloud/Nomaduntu): the Ubuntu
  counterpart, using HashiCorp's apt repo and [systemd](https://systemd.io).
- [`cosmonautical.nomadable`](https://github.com/Cosmonautical-Cloud/Nomadable): the parent
  playbook. It routes each host to Nomadintosh or Nomaduntu based on its OS, so a mixed macOS and
  Ubuntu cluster deploys in a single run.

The [Nomad jobs](https://github.com/Jellify-Music/Nomad-Jobs) themselves are defined in
[Terraform](https://www.terraform.io), and both the Ansible playbooks and Terraform configs get run
by [Semaphore](https://semaphoreui.com), which (of course) runs as a Nomad job on the very cluster
it deploys to.

### Apple + IBM sounds familiar...

If you're keeping track: Nomad, Consul, and Terraform come from
[HashiCorp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HashiCorp), which IBM
[acquired in 2025](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2025-02-27-ibm-completes-acquisition-of-hashicorp,-creates-comprehensive,-end-to-end-hybrid-cloud-platform),
and Ansible comes from [Red Hat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Hat), which IBM acquired back
in 2019. So my homelab is _technically_ Apple hardware running IBM software.

Just missing Motorola in the mix and it's like [AIM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIM_alliance)
never dissolved.

## Roll Out the Barrels

The jobs I run on the rack can be broken up into tiers, based on how critical they are.

- Tier 1 (Core Infrastructure):
  - [Postgres](https://www.postgresql.org) (Patroni, using Consul as its DCS)
    - A separate Nomad job takes a
      [`pg_dumpall`](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/app-pg-dumpall.html) every 6 hours
  - [Redis](https://redis.io) +
    [Sentinel](https://redis.io/docs/latest/operate/oss_and_stack/management/sentinel/)
  - [SeaweedFS](https://github.com/seaweedfs/seaweedfs) (3 masters, 3 volume servers, a filer, NFS
    backup)
  - [Traefik](https://traefik.io) + [Fail2ban](https://github.com/fail2ban/fail2ban)
  - [OpenLDAP](https://www.openldap.org) + [Keycloak](https://www.keycloak.org)
  - Semaphore
    - Runs the Ansible and Terraform that deploy everything else, including itself
- Tier 2 (Critical Applications):
  - [Nextcloud](https://nextcloud.com)
    - Primary app is deployed as a Nomad service
    - Nomad batch jobs are deployed for Nextcloud tasks to run on schedule
      - Nextcloud's cron
      - preview generation
      - NFS share scans
      - S3 backups to [Backblaze](https://www.backblaze.com/cloud-storage)
  - Jellyfin
    - A native `.app` that uses Apple's
      [VideoToolbox](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/videotoolbox) for
      [hardware accelerated transcoding](https://jellyfin.org/docs/general/post-install/transcoding/hardware-acceleration/)
    - [Dispatcharr](https://github.com/Dispatcharr/Dispatcharr) acts as an
      [HDHomeRun](https://www.silicondust.com) tuner for Live TV
  - [AudioMuse-AI](https://github.com/NeptuneHub/AudioMuse-AI)
    - A native `.app` for music sonic analysis, leveraging CoreML
  - Ollama + [Open WebUI](https://openwebui.com)
- Tier 3 (Secondary Applications):
  - [RomM](https://github.com/rommapp/romm)
  - Guacamole
  - [Seerr](https://github.com/seerr-team/seerr) for media requests
  - GitHub Actions runners for Jellify (more on those below)

Some jobs are `raw_exec`, running on bare macOS (Jellyfin, Postgres/Patroni, Nextcloud's php-fpm,
AudioMuse), while others are Linux containers through Apple `container` via my
[`nomad-driver-container`](https://github.com/anultravioletaurora/nomad-driver-container) plugin.

## Shut Up and Drive

All of this would be a very expensive space heater if nobody used it. Cosmonautical is mostly used
by me, my friends, and my family, and Jellyfin sees the most use by a mile.

### Jellyfin and Co.

Jellyfin is where I watch and listen to damn near everything. Movies, shows, and Live TV through
Dispatcharr all come from the cluster, and when someone wants something that isn't in the library
yet, they ask for it in Seerr instead of texting me. Since Jellyfin runs natively on macOS,
transcoding goes through the Media Engine, so a friend streaming on hotel Wi-Fi doesn't bring the
Mac to its knees.

Music is obviously the part I care about the most. It's how Jellify gets dogfooded: I stream
self-hosted music through Jellify every day across various setups, so I'm constantly on the hunt for
bugs or imagining new features.

#### Sonic Analysis with AudioMuse-AI

Streaming services have one big advantage over a self-hosted library, and that's recommendations.
Spotify knows that a song is upbeat, or mellow, or sounds a lot like another song. A folder of FLACs
on a NAS doesn't know any of that.

[AudioMuse-AI](https://github.com/NeptuneHub/AudioMuse-AI) closes that gap. It analyzes the audio of
every track in the Jellyfin libraries, building up a sonic profile of the whole collection. It runs
as a native `.app` on macOS, so the analysis happens on Apple Silicon with CoreML, and the results
live in Postgres and Redis alongside everything else.

AudioMuse-AI's [Jellyfin plugin](https://github.com/NeptuneHub/audiomuse-ai-plugin) exposes all of
it through Jellyfin itself. The plugin swaps Jellyfin's Instant Mix for one built on sonic
similarity, so any Jellyfin client gets smarter mixes without changing a thing. It also adds
endpoints for similar tracks and artists, plain-language search ("upbeat summer songs"), and
chat-generated playlists.

To make those easy to use in Jellify, I wrote a typed client for the plugin,
[`@jellify-music/audiomuse-ai-jellyfin-sdk`](https://github.com/Jellify-Music/AudioMuse-AI-Jellyfin-SDK),
built on top of the [Jellyfin TypeScript SDK](https://github.com/jellyfin/jellyfin-sdk-typescript).
Jellify checks if the plugin is installed on your server, and if it is, you can describe a playlist
and have AudioMuse-AI build it from your own library. All of that analysis runs on hardware I own,
against music I own.

## Jellify Actions

The Jellify datacenter exists to run GitHub Actions for Jellify. A Nomad
[`system` job](https://developer.hashicorp.com/nomad/docs/concepts/scheduling/schedulers) puts one
ephemeral runner on every node tagged as a runner. Each runner takes exactly one job, wipes its work
folder, and registers a fresh replacement, so no build ever sees the last one's leftovers.

Any action that doesn't need a Mac lands on x64 Linux runners from the same Nomad job. These
include:

- [Jest](https://jestjs.io) unit tests on every branch push
- JS bundle and Android APK builds on PRs
- Publishing OTA updates, and cleaning up the per-PR OTA branches once a PR closes

Fork PRs get GitHub's hosted runners instead, so fork code never runs on my hardware.

### Maestro Testing

[Maestro](https://maestro.mobile.dev) end-to-end tests are the headline act, and the reason those
runners need to be Macs in the first place: the iOS Simulator only runs on macOS.

Every PR, plus a nightly run, builds a release copy of the app and drives it through the full UI
flow on _both_ an iOS Simulator and an
[Android emulator](https://developer.android.com/studio/run/emulator): setup and login, home, quick
actions, library, search, discover, settings, and the player and queue. The same flows run on both
platforms, with small helper subflows papering over the differences (iOS has no back button, for
one).

The tests run as `raw_exec` straight on macOS, using the Xcode, simulators, and Android SDK that
Ansible provisions, so there's no VM between the emulator and the hardware. When a run finishes,
screenshots, logs, and the UI hierarchy get uploaded as artifacts, and the result gets posted to
[Discord](https://discord.gg/yf8fBatktn). If a PR breaks the player or the queue, I find out before
it ships, not from a bug report.

### Release Notes

The Jellify datacenter is mostly walled off from Cosmonautical. There's one exception, though:
release notes.

Every Jellify release, both store builds and over-the-air updates, gets its notes written by an LLM
running on the cluster. The pipeline is a
[composite GitHub Action](https://github.com/Jellify-Music/App/tree/main/.github/actions/generate-release-notes)
with a few steps:

1. **Work out the tag.** A store release counts from the previous store release, so people updating
   from the App Store or Play Store see everything since their last update, including anything that
   already shipped over the air. An OTA update counts from the previous tag of either kind, so it
   only covers what's new.
2. **Collect the changes.** A script walks every commit between the two tags and asks the GitHub API
   which merged PR each one belongs to. Then it uses [GraphQL](https://docs.github.com/en/graphql)
   (the only API that tells you which issues a PR closes) to pull in the closed issues, and it
   collects every contributor who isn't a bot. Version bumps and merge commits are skipped, since
   they don't say anything about the release.
3. **Write the narrative.** The PR titles, closed issues, and any commits that didn't come from a PR
   go to [`gemma4:12b`](https://ollama.com/library/gemma4), running on Ollama and served through the
   OpenAI-compatible API in Open WebUI. The prompt asks for a short summary and three highlight
   bullet points. For OTA updates, it also mentions that the update downloads the next time the app
   is opened.
4. **Ship it.** The narrative goes to [TestFlight](https://developer.apple.com/testflight/) with the
   iOS build, and to Discord through a
   [webhook](https://support.discord.com/hc/en-us/articles/228383668-Intro-to-Webhooks) that pings
   the release role. The GitHub release gets the narrative too, followed by the list of closed
   issues, merged PRs, and a thank-you to everyone who contributed.

So every Jellify release goes from merged PRs to TestFlight and a Discord announcement without me
writing a word, all powered by a Mac Mini in my rack.

## Standard Operating Procedures

With vulnerabilities landing faster than ever, I've had plenty of practice running through patching
procedures. Patches go in one of two ways: Homebrew via Ansible, or Nomad jobs via Semaphore.

### Homebrewed

Homebrew upgrades get deployed by rerunning the
[Nomadintosh](https://github.com/Cosmonautical-Cloud/Nomadintosh) playbook. Every run upgrades
Nomad, Consul, and the rest of the Homebrew packages in my inventory to their latest versions, so
patching a host is as simple as running the playbook again.

Upgrading is the easy part. Homebrew swaps the binary on disk, but anything already running keeps
using the old one, so the playbook also handles the restarts:

- **Nomad and Consul** restart one host at a time, and the playbook waits for the agent and the
  cluster to report healthy before moving on to the next one. A run that upgrades every server never
  takes more than one of them out of quorum.
- **Nomad jobs** running directly on macOS (`raw_exec`) get restarted afterwards, again one at a
  time. They'd otherwise keep running the old binaries, under an executor started by the old `nomad`
  binary that Homebrew has since deleted. Don't ask me how I know this (my Postgres replicas once
  ended up ~23 GB behind for days without me knowing lol).

The one step that can't be automated is the Local Network prompt. After an upgrade, I hop onto each
Mac via Guacamole and click "Allow" for the freshly signed binaries.

### Nomad Life

Version bumps for Nomad jobs come from [Renovate](https://github.com/renovatebot/renovate), which
opens a PR against my Terraform whenever a new release of an app ships. Once I merge it to `main`,
Semaphore runs `terraform plan` and then holds the change for my approval before `terraform apply`
rolls it out to the cluster. GitOps FTW.

## Power Levels

So, how did that curiosity about performance per watt pan out? Pretty well, actually.

Before the Macs, my three-node Harvester cluster (each node with a Ryzen 7 5700G and an RTX 4060)
idled somewhere in the mid 200Ws, and could easily pull around 400W once I threw some real load at
it. Those numbers come from remembering what my UPS showed, not careful measurement, but they're in
the right ballpark.

The new rack (affectionately referred to ass "Mac Rack Mini Pro") has twice as many nodes and still
comes in well under that. It idles in the mid 100Ws, and under load it settles in the mid 200Ws,
about where the old cluster used to _idle_.

That's added up over time. Since making the switch, my power usage has dropped by around 75-125kWh a
month. Twice the nodes, less power.

## Should You Eat the Forbidden Fruit?

Would I recommend it? For most people, as you might've guessed, absolutely not. If you don't _need_
macOS, a cheaper Linux box with discrete graphics for hardware acceleration will get you to the same
place with far less friction.

But if you're a die-hard Apple fan, appreciate funky hardware, or your use case actually requires
Apple's software like Xcode, the seeds you sow will yield a nice harvest. The Minis are whisper
quiet, sip power, and running them as a cluster is entirely possible.

You need to go in with your eyes open. They aren't cheap, and macOS will fight you every step of the
way. No matter how much you automate, be prepared to remote into the desktop every now and again to
click through whatever prompt it's decided to throw at you this time.
